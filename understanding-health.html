Health, Formally Defined · A guided reading

Understanding
Health.

I want to invite you into a question that seems familiar until we try to answer it precisely: what does it mean for something to be healthy?

Together, we'll move from everyday examples to the underlying mathematics, and eventually to the difficult work of measurement. I'll explain each idea as we reach it. You don't need a background in formal mathematics to begin.

14 lessons publishedBased on the five-paper Health seriesWritten for curious readers

The approach

We'll build the idea before we ask the mathematics to carry it.

At the center of the research is a way of thinking about health that includes both the organization present now and its capacity to continue under specified conditions.

The mathematics gives that idea a precise form. The empirical work asks which parts of it can actually be observed, reconstructed, calibrated, and tested. I want to make both parts accessible without blurring what has been proved and what still needs evidence.

A note on the lessons

These are guided explanations of the published research, written in my voice for readers encountering it for the first time. Every lesson leads back to its scientific source. The formal examples establish mathematical properties under declared assumptions; applications to living systems require their own evidence.

Lesson 1 · Paper I · Start here

What does it actually mean for something to be healthy?

Two forests. One apparently thriving, another visibly damaged. A simple distinction between how a system looks today and what it can still do tomorrow leads us to the central equation.

Lesson 2 · Paper I · Continue reading

What is a viable continuation?

Begin with a stressed forest and follow the possibilities. Learn why a future may be lawful without being viable, and why the capacity we observe can be simpler than the histories behind it.

↗ 03

Lesson 3 · Paper I · Continue reading

Can two systems have different capacities but still be equally healthy?

Four capacity sets, two health requirements, and a way to group the information each question can see. Explore what changes when I introduce a new requirement.

↗ 04

Lesson 4 · Paper I · Complete the foundation

Why having the capacity to survive is not the same as being robust

A successful pathway is only part of a system's story. Explore why robustness and prospective Health can require information that success-only or present-only observations do not retain.

↗ 05

Lesson 5 · Paper II · A new context

How can the same system be healthy in one context and unhealthy in another?

Two ways a health judgment can change: the capacity available to a system and the requirements licensed to evaluate it.

↗ 06

Lesson 6 · Paper II · Transport and information

How can we carry information about health from one context to another?

Follow a forest state related to two different targets, see why the full target capacity may be indeterminate, and learn when the health-visible answer can still be carried across contexts.

↗ 07

Lesson 7 · Paper II · Open-system support

Can a system be healthy if it depends on something outside itself to survive?

Four formal support conditions, two horizons, and a striking result: identical short-term capacities can conceal different long-term dependence. Finish Paper II with a clear view of support and continued organization.

↗ 08

Lesson 8 · Paper III · Begin with measurement

How do we know whether a measurement contains enough information to determine health?

Two systems with identical success-possible answers but different stipulated probabilities lead us to the principal sufficiency theorem: every correct representation must preserve the distinctions demanded by its questions.

↗ 09

Lesson 9 · Paper III · Exact transport

Why more information can sometimes make prediction harder

Understand the asymmetry between enriching source information and demanding a finer future target. Follow the least sufficient target theorem and its exact finite forest witness.

↗ 10

Lesson 10 · Paper III · Probability and viable cost

Is being able to survive the same as being likely to survive?

Learn why possibility, probability, and affordability are distinct—and how one success-probability number or an attained minimum cost can preserve every answer to a declared question family.

↗ 11

Lesson 11 · Paper III · Control and robustness

Having a way to survive is not the same as being able to control your survival

From an available successful future to policy-generated recovery, multiple routes, and failure-aware robustness. Complete our guided introduction to Paper III.

↗ 12

Lesson 12 · Paper IV · Information provenance

How information gets lost—and why it matters for health

Five formal forest states, four projected capacity classes, and three declared Health-profile classes. Discover when information is erased and when it is retained but unused.

↗ 13

Lesson 13 · Paper IV · Conservative enrichment

Can we improve a measurement without invalidating what it told us before?

Exact projection, adequacy pullback, and the theorem that preserves established Health judgments while revealing new distinctions between recovery and adaptation.

↗ 14

Lesson 14 · Paper V · Incomplete observations

What happens when we don't actually know the system's present state?

Enter the Harvard Forest study, reconstruct an uncertain present from incomplete census records, and confront the limits of cohort compression, calibration, and identification.
